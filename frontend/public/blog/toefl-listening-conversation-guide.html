TOEFL Listening Conversation: Tracking Problem, Reason, and Outcome

Conversations — usually between a student and a professor, advisor, or campus staff member — almost always follow the same underlying shape: someone has a problem or a question, it gets discussed, and it ends with some kind of resolution or next step. Questions are built around exactly that structure.

The pattern behind almost every conversation

  1. Why the conversation is happening — usually stated directly in the first 20–30 seconds.
  2. The problem or request in more detail — often with some back-and-forth or a complication.
  3. Options discussed — the staff member frequently offers more than one possible solution.
  4. What the student decides, or what happens next — this is a very common final question.

A note template built around this shape

Note template
WHO: student + [role]
WHY: →
OPTIONS MENTIONED: 1. ___ 2. ___
STUDENT'S REACTION / DECISION: →

Filling this in as you listen keeps you focused on the four things that questions actually target, instead of trying to transcribe the whole exchange.

Watch for the student's tone at the end

A common question type asks about the student's attitude by the end of the conversation — relieved, still frustrated, undecided. This is rarely stated outright; it's usually audible in the last few lines, so don't mentally check out once the "facts" have been given.

If the staff member gives more than one option, expect a question about which one the student ultimately prefers, or why one option doesn't work for them.
