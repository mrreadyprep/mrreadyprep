TOEFL® vs IELTS: Which English Test Should You Take? (2026 Guide)

Most universities, visa programs, and employers that ask for an English proficiency score accept either TOEFL iBT® or IELTS Academic — so for most people, the choice isn't about which test is "better." It's about which format fits how you actually perform under pressure, and which one your specific target program prefers. Here's how the two compare in 2026, and a simple way to decide.

The biggest difference: how Speaking actually works

This is the one that matters most day-to-day. On the TOEFL, you're alone with a microphone — you read or listen to a prompt, then speak your answer into a recording, with no one on the other end reacting in real time. On the IELTS, you're in a real conversation with a trained examiner who asks follow-up questions and responds to what you say.

Some people do noticeably better with TOEFL's format because there's no live person to feel nervous in front of, and you can fully focus on organizing your answer. Others find IELTS easier because a real conversation feels more natural than talking to a screen, and a good examiner can help pace the exchange. If you already know which of these describes you from past exams or interviews, that alone can be a good reason to pick one test over the other.

Test length has changed a lot for 2026

The TOEFL iBT went through a major redesign that took effect in January 2026: the whole test is now well under two hours, down from the roughly two-hour format used previously, and Reading and Listening are now multistage adaptive — the questions you get in the second stage of each section depend on how you performed in the first stage. IELTS hasn't changed its structure the same way and remains a longer, fixed-format test at around 2 hours 45 minutes.

If a long test day is genuinely draining for your concentration, the shorter 2026 TOEFL format is worth factoring in.

Scoring: two different scales

As of the 2026 update, TOEFL iBT reports each of the four sections — Reading, Listening, Speaking, Writing — on a new 1–6 band scale, with your overall score being the average of the four. Score reports during the 2026–2028 transition period also show the familiar 0–120 total alongside the new bands, so older score requirements you might see referenced online are still meaningful for a while yet. IELTS uses its own long-standing 0–9 band scale, scored in 0.5-point increments per section and overall.

Because the two scales don't map onto each other one-to-one, both ETS (TOEFL) and IELTS publish official score comparison tables. If a specific score is a hard requirement somewhere, check the official comparison directly rather than relying on a rough rule of thumb — conversions you'll see quoted casually online aren't always precise.

Which one does my target school or visa program accept?

Both tests are broadly accepted by universities and immigration programs worldwide, and the gap between them has narrowed a lot over the past decade. That said, requirements vary by country, by individual institution, and sometimes by specific program within the same university — some explicitly require one over the other, and some accept either with different minimum scores for each. This is genuinely worth 10 minutes of direct research: check the admissions or visa page for your specific target before committing your prep time to one test.

A simple way to decide

  • Your target program requires a specific test. That settles it — don't spend prep time on the other one.
  • You get anxious talking to people you don't know, or you think better with a moment to plan silently. TOEFL's recorded, no-interviewer Speaking format tends to suit this better.
  • You'd rather have a real back-and-forth conversation than talk into a microphone alone. IELTS's live examiner interview tends to suit this better.
  • You want the shorter test day. The 2026 TOEFL format is meaningfully shorter than IELTS.
  • Neither factor above is decisive for you. Try a free sample question from each section of both tests — the one that feels more natural after 20 minutes of hands-on practice is usually the better bet.
Whichever test you choose, the preparation that actually moves your score is the same in spirit: realistic timed practice on every question type, honest feedback on your writing and speaking, and enough repetition that the format itself stops being the hard part on test day.

If TOEFL iBT is the one you're taking, practice every section — Reading, Listening, Writing, and Speaking — with instant, detailed feedback.
