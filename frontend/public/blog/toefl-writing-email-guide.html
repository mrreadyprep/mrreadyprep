How to Write a TOEFL iBT Email Response That Scores 4+ (With Example)

The TOEFL iBT Writing section includes a task where you read a short email and write a reply. It looks simple, but it's one of the easiest tasks to lose points on — not because the English is hard, but because most responses are too short, skip part of what was asked, or don't sound like a real email. Here's exactly what raters are looking for, and a full example you can study.

What the task actually asks for

You'll see a short email from someone — a professor, an administrator, a company — describing a situation and usually asking you to do 2–3 specific things: answer a question, make a request, propose a solution, or explain a decision. Your reply needs to address every one of those points, not just the general topic.

The single biggest score-killer is answering the topic in general terms while skipping one of the specific things you were asked to do. Raters check this closely — reread the prompt right before you finish writing and confirm every bullet point is covered.

What raters are actually scoring

Based on the official scoring guidelines, your response is judged on a combination of these areas:

  • Task completion — did you address everything the email asked, not just part of it?
  • Organization & format — does it read like a real email, with a greeting, a clear body, and a closing?
  • Development — is each point actually explained, or just mentioned in one short sentence?
  • Grammar & mechanics — consistent capitalization, punctuation, no fragments or run-ons.
  • Vocabulary & tone — appropriately formal/professional word choice for an email.

Notice that grammar is only one of five things being judged. A grammatically perfect response that's 40 words long and doesn't explain anything will still score low — length and development matter just as much as accuracy.

A simple structure that works for almost every prompt

  1. Greeting — "Dear Mr. Alvarez," or "Dear Professor Kim,"
  2. Opening line — briefly acknowledge why you're writing.
  3. One paragraph per point — address each thing you were asked about, and explain your reasoning (use words like "because," "since," "for example" — this is what separates a 3 from a 4-5).
  4. Closing — a short wrap-up line, then "Best regards," and your name.

Full example

Prompt (summarized)
A community center director emails you saying the center is organizing a weekend beach cleanup and needs volunteers. She asks you to: (1) confirm whether you can attend, (2) suggest one way to encourage more people to join, and (3) ask any question you have about the event.
Weak response (~45 words, would score low)
Dear Ms. Alvarez, I got your email about the beach cleanup. I think it's a good idea and I can help. Maybe you can post about it online. What time does it start? Thank you.
Best, Mehmet

This response technically touches all three points, but each one is a single, undeveloped sentence. It doesn't explain why the idea would work, doesn't confirm attendance clearly, and reads more like a text message than a professional email.

Stronger response (~135 words)
Dear Ms. Alvarez,

Thank you for reaching out about the weekend beach cleanup. I would be glad to confirm that I can attend — Saturday morning works well with my schedule, and I'm looking forward to contributing.

To encourage more people to join, I would suggest partnering with a few local businesses to offer volunteers a small discount or free coffee afterward. Since many people are motivated by a simple incentive, this could noticeably increase turnout, especially among students who might not otherwise prioritize a Saturday morning activity.

One question I have is whether we should bring our own gloves and trash bags, or if the center will be providing supplies on-site. Please let me know so I can plan accordingly.

Best regards,
Mehmet

The difference isn't vocabulary — it's development. Every point gets a full explanation, the tone is consistently professional, and it reads like something a real person would send.

Three mistakes to avoid

  • Writing under 100 words. There's no hard minimum, but very short responses almost never develop their points enough to score well.
  • Skipping the greeting or closing. It's a small thing, but it's part of "organization & format," and it's free points if you remember it.
  • Restating the prompt instead of responding to it. "You said there will be a beach cleanup" isn't a response — react to it, decide something, explain your reasoning.
