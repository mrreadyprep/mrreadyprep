TOEFL Speaking: How to Structure a 45-Second Interview Answer

In the TOEFL iBT Speaking interview task, you get a short prompt about a familiar topic — your preferences, experiences, or opinions — 15 seconds to prepare, and 45 seconds to speak. That's not much time, and most people either freeze for the first 10 seconds or run out of things to say halfway through. Having a fixed structure ready before you walk in fixes both problems.

The timing breakdown

TimeWhat to do
0–15 secPreparation. Don't write full sentences — jot 2–3 keywords for your main points.
0–5 secAnswer the question directly (your opinion/choice/preference).
5–35 secGive two reasons, each with a specific example or detail.
35–45 secA short closing sentence that wraps up your answer.

A structure you can reuse for almost any prompt

  1. Direct answer — "I would prefer to study alone" / "My favorite subject in school was history."
  2. Reason 1 + example — explain why, then make it concrete with a specific detail or personal experience.
  3. Reason 2 + example — a second, different reason, same pattern.
  4. Closing line — "That's why I think X is the better option for me."
You don't need to fill every second with new information. A shorter answer that's clearly organized and fully developed scores better than a longer one that trails off or repeats itself.

Full example

Prompt
Some people prefer to study alone, while others prefer to study in a group. Which do you prefer, and why?
Example answer (~45 seconds spoken)
I personally prefer to study alone rather than in a group. There are two main reasons for this.

First, when I study alone, I can go at my own pace. For example, if I'm reviewing a difficult math concept, I can spend as much time as I need without worrying about slowing other people down.

Second, I find it easier to concentrate without distractions. When I studied in a group last semester, we often ended up talking about unrelated things instead of actually working.

So for me, studying alone is more effective, even though group study can be useful for some people.

Notice the pattern: direct answer, reason with a concrete example, second reason with a concrete example, short close. Every sentence has a job — nothing is filler.

Filler phrases that hurt more than they help

"Well, that's a really interesting question, let me think about it for a moment..." feels natural in conversation, but in a 45-second response it eats up time you don't have and adds nothing to your score. Skip straight to your answer.

What raters are listening for

  • Delivery — clear pronunciation, natural pacing, not too fast or full of long pauses.
  • Language use — a mix of simple and more complex sentence structures, appropriate vocabulary.
  • Topic development — a clear, complete answer with specific supporting details, not just a general statement.
