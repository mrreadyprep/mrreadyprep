TOEFL vs IELTS 2026: Which Test Should You Take?

If a program you're applying to accepts both TOEFL and IELTS, the choice usually isn't about which test is "harder" overall -- it's about which format plays to your actual strengths. Here's an honest, side-by-side comparison to help you decide.

Where each test is genuinely harder

TOEFL tends to be harder on:

  • Speaking without a real conversation partner — you respond to a prompt alone, against a timer, with no follow-up questions to react to.
  • Reading variety — three distinct task types (including Complete the Words, which has no multiple-choice options to fall back on).
  • Strict, per-task time pressure across every section.

IELTS tends to be harder on:

  • Speaking under direct social pressure — a live examiner, in real time, which some test takers find more nerve-wracking than a webcam.
  • Writing Task 1 — describing a chart, graph, or process is a skill most students haven't practiced outside test prep.
  • Listening focus — the audio runs continuously with no built-in pauses to catch up on notes.

Who tends to do better on which test

Lean toward TOEFL if…

You think and write more naturally in a structured, academic register; you're comfortable being scored by a mix of AI and trained human raters rather than a single live examiner; and you're applying mainly to universities in the US or Canada, where TOEFL is the more common default.

Lean toward IELTS if…

You communicate more naturally in conversation than in formal writing; you'd rather talk to a real person than a webcam for the Speaking section; and you're applying mainly to universities in the UK, Australia, or continental Europe, where IELTS is frequently preferred or required.

University acceptance: check the specific program

As a general pattern, most universities in the US and Canada default to TOEFL, and most in the UK, Australia, Ireland, and New Zealand default to IELTS — but a large and growing number of universities worldwide accept both, and some accept only one for specific programs (law and medicine schools are more likely to have stricter requirements). Don't rely on a general rule: check the admissions page for the exact program you're applying to, since the accepted-test policy is set at the institution or even department level.

There is no single official conversion table between TOEFL and IELTS — different institutions and test-prep organizations publish slightly different equivalency charts. Treat the numbers above as a rough estimate, and when a specific score matters (a hard admissions cutoff, for instance), confirm directly with the university rather than relying on any conversion.

Should you prepare for both?

Usually not. The two tests share enough underlying skills (grammar, vocabulary range, organization, listening comprehension) that solid preparation for one carries over significantly to the other. Preparing for both from scratch, in parallel, tends to split your practice time without a proportional benefit. The two cases where it's worth reconsidering:

  1. You're applying to universities in two regions with different default tests (say, both the US and the UK), and neither accepts the other's score for your specific programs.
  2. You've already taken one test, scored just below your target, and diagnostic practice on the other format suggests it plays more to your strengths.
