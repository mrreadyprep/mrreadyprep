TOEFL Reading: How to Answer Vocabulary, Detail, and Inference Questions

The TOEFL Reading section tests three things at once: how fast you read, how well you understand what you read, and how well you can find information under time pressure. Most of the score difference between test-takers isn't vocabulary size — it's knowing exactly what each question type is asking for and where to look.

The main question types (and how to attack each one)

1. Vocabulary-in-context

You're given a word from the passage and asked what it most nearly means. The trap here is picking the dictionary definition you already know instead of the meaning that fits this specific sentence. Always reread the sentence with each answer choice substituted in — the one that keeps the sentence's meaning and tone intact is correct, even if it's not the definition you'd expect.

2. Detail / fact questions

These ask about something explicitly stated in the passage ("According to the passage, why did X happen?"). The information is always there — your job is to locate it quickly, not to reason it out. Skim for the paragraph the question is likely referencing (question order usually follows passage order) rather than rereading the whole passage.

3. Inference questions

These ask what the passage implies but doesn't say directly. The correct answer is a small, logical step from stated facts — not a big leap, and not something obvious that's already stated. If an answer choice restates something the passage says word-for-word, it's testing a detail question, not an inference — that's usually a wrong choice here.

4. Sentence simplification

You're shown a complex sentence and asked which answer choice best captures its essential meaning, without leaving out important information or adding inaccurate details. Cross out any option that changes the meaning, adds something not in the original, or drops a key idea.

5. Insert-text questions

You're given a sentence and asked where it best fits into the passage (marked with squares). Look for connecting words in the sentence you're inserting — "however," "this," "as a result" — these are your biggest clues, since they signal what kind of sentence needs to come right before it.

A structural pattern worth knowing: most academic passages open with a general claim, then spend each following paragraph supporting it with one specific type of evidence. Once you spot this shape early, detail and inference questions get much faster to answer, because you already know roughly where each kind of information lives.

Time management

StepApproximate time
Skim the passage for structure (don't read every word yet)1–2 min
Answer questions, referring back to the passage as neededMost of your time
Final check on any flagged/uncertain answersRemaining time

Don't try to memorize the passage before looking at the questions — reading academic passages twice, once generally and once question-by-question, is usually faster overall than trying to absorb every detail on the first pass.

Building the underlying skill

Reading speed and vocabulary range are built gradually, not memorized overnight. Short, frequent practice — a few vocabulary-in-context questions and one short passage a day — beats occasional long study sessions, because you're training pattern recognition, not just recalling facts.
