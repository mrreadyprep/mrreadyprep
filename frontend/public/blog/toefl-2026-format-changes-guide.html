TOEFL® iBT 2026 Format: The Complete Guide to Every Change

ETS launched a redesigned TOEFL iBT on January 21, 2026 — the biggest change to the exam in over a decade. The test is shorter, the task types are different, and the score report itself now looks different. If you last researched the TOEFL a year or two ago, a lot of what you remember no longer applies. Here's exactly what changed, section by section.

The short version

WhatBefore Jan 2026From Jan 2026
Total length~2 hours~90 minutes
Scoring scale0–120 (0–30 per section)1–6 per section, 0.5-point increments, CEFR-aligned
Reading & ListeningFixed set of questions for everyoneMultistage adaptive — difficulty adjusts to your performance
Task styleMostly academic passages and lecturesMix of academic content plus everyday emails, notices, and conversations
Score reports issued between 2026 and 2028 show both the new 1–6 band and the familiar 0–120 number side by side, so admissions offices have time to adjust. After that transition window, only the 1–6 band will appear.

Reading — now 30 minutes, three task types

Reading moved from two long academic passages to a mix of three distinct task types, and now adapts to your ability as you go:

  • Complete the Words — fill in missing letters in a short passage. This tests vocabulary and spelling recognition directly, without multiple-choice options to lean on. Full strategy guide →
  • Read in Daily Life — answer questions about a notice, schedule, ad, or short email, exactly like something you'd actually encounter on a campus. Full strategy guide →
  • Academic Passage — the closest task to the old-format Reading section: a university-level text with comprehension, inference, and vocabulary-in-context questions. Full strategy guide →

Listening — now 29 minutes, four task types

  • Listen and Choose a Response — hear a single spoken line and pick the most appropriate reply. Tests tone and function, not just literal meaning. Full strategy guide →
  • Listen to a Conversation — a student-staff exchange with questions about the problem, reason, and outcome. Full strategy guide →
  • Listen to an Announcement — a short campus announcement; questions target what, when, and what to do next. Full strategy guide →
  • Listen to an Academic Talk — a mini-lecture testing whether you can follow structure and track supporting examples. Full strategy guide →

Like Reading, Listening is now multistage adaptive: your performance on the first set of items shapes how challenging the next set is, which is part of how ETS shortened the section without losing accuracy.

Writing — now 23 minutes, three task types

  • Build a Sentence — arrange scrambled words from a conversation into a correct sentence. A fast, direct grammar check. Full strategy guide →
  • Write an Email — compose a reply to a realistic situation (a professor, a landlord, a classmate). Scored on task completion, organization, and language use. Full strategy guide →
  • Write for an Academic Discussion — post a response in a simulated online class discussion, replacing the old integrated essay. Full strategy guide →

Speaking — now 8 minutes, two task types

  • Listen and Repeat — repeat short spoken sentences with a visual cue, up to 45 seconds each. Tests pronunciation, stress, and rhythm directly. Full strategy guide →
  • Take an Interview — answer 3–4 interview-style questions with no prep time, about 45 seconds per response. Closest to a real conversation of any TOEFL task, before or after the redesign. Full strategy guide →
Why Speaking shrank the most

The old Speaking section had four tasks built around reading or listening to a passage first, then responding — a format that mostly tested memory and note-taking under pressure as much as speech itself. Listen and Repeat and Take an Interview cut straight to pronunciation, fluency, and spontaneous language use, which is why the section is now a third of its old length but arguably harder to fake your way through.

The CEFR alignment is the real point of this change — it lets a university or employer compare a TOEFL band directly against IELTS, Cambridge English, or any other CEFR-referenced test, instead of needing a separate conversion chart. If a program's admissions page still lists a target as an old 0–120 number, the table above gets you a reasonable estimate of the equivalent 1–6 band.

What this means for how you should prepare

Three practical takeaways follow directly from the redesign:

  1. Practice by task type, not by "section." Since each section is now a mix of genuinely different task formats, drilling only "Reading" or only "Speaking" in the abstract is less useful than practicing each task type on its own — Complete the Words is a different skill from an Academic Passage, even though both are "Reading."
  2. Don't train on old-format materials alone. Practice tests built around the pre-2026 four-passage Reading section or four-task Speaking section no longer reflect what you'll see on test day. Make sure whatever you're practicing with has been updated for the current format.
  3. Get comfortable with adaptive difficulty. Because Reading and Listening now adjust to your performance in real time, there's less value in memorizing question patterns and more value in building genuine, consistent accuracy — the test is actively probing for your real level rather than asking a fixed set of questions.
