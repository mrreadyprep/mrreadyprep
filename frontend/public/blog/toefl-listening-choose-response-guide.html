TOEFL Listening Choose-Response: How to Catch What a Speaker Really Means

Choose-Response items are short — a single line or a brief exchange — but they move fast, and the correct answer usually depends on how something is said, not just what the words literally mean.

Listen for function, not just content

A short line of speech can be a complaint, a suggestion, a polite refusal, or sarcasm — and the words themselves often don't tell you which. Tone, pacing, and small words like "well," "actually," or "I guess" are doing most of the work. Train yourself to ask "what is this person doing by saying this?" rather than just "what are the words?"

Example
"I guess I could try finishing it tonight..."

Literally, this states a plan. In tone, the hesitation ("I guess") usually signals reluctance — the speaker is being asked to do something they'd rather not do, not confidently announcing a plan. The correct answer is often about the speaker's attitude, not the literal statement.

Don't overanalyze — the answer is usually immediate

Because these clips are short, the correct answer is almost always about the immediate, most natural interpretation of what you just heard — not a deep or unusual reading. If you find yourself building an elaborate explanation for why an unlikely answer choice could technically be correct, that's a signal to go back to the more obvious option.

Practice the sound of these functions, not just vocabulary

The best preparation is exposure — repeatedly hearing what hesitation, sarcasm, surprise, or polite disagreement actually sound like in natural speech, until recognizing them becomes automatic instead of something you have to consciously work out under time pressure.

If two answer choices both seem grammatically possible, pick the one that matches the speaker's tone, not just the literal content of the sentence.
