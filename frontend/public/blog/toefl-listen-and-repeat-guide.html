TOEFL Listen & Repeat: Building Natural Pronunciation and Rhythm

Listen & Repeat is built around a simple idea: hear a phrase in a realistic, everyday scene, then repeat it back matching not just the words but the rhythm behind them. It sounds basic, but it's one of the fastest ways to build natural-sounding speech, because it trains your ear and your mouth together instead of separately.

Match the rhythm, not just the words

English is a stress-timed language — some words in a sentence are stressed and stretched out, while others (like "a," "the," "to") are compressed and almost swallowed. Getting the words right but the rhythm flat is what makes speech sound unnatural, even when it's grammatically correct.

Example
"I was going to CALL you, but I forGOT."

The capitalized syllables carry the stress — that up-and-down pattern is what native rhythm actually sounds like. Flattening every syllable equally is one of the most common patterns that makes speech harder to follow, even when every word is correct.

Content words get stressed; function words don't

As a rough rule: nouns, main verbs, adjectives, and adverbs (the words carrying meaning) get stressed. Articles, prepositions, and auxiliary verbs (the small grammatical glue words) get compressed. Listening for this pattern — and then reproducing it — is the core skill this task is training.

Don't rush the repetition

The instinct is to repeat quickly to "get it over with," but speed without accurate rhythm doesn't build the skill. Slow down enough to actually match the stress pattern you just heard — speed comes naturally later, once the pattern itself is automatic.

Realistic, scenario-based phrases (ordering at a counter, asking for directions, a quick clarifying question) are more useful practice than isolated word lists, because stress and rhythm patterns shift depending on context.
